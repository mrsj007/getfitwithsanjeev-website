What BMI does not tell you

BMI does not measure muscle, bone, fat distribution, pregnancy or ethnicity differences. Muscular people and some older adults can be misclassified. It is one number among many. Check with a doctor for health decisions.

If you are pregnant, speak with your healthcare provider about weight and activity rather than relying on BMI alone.

Understanding BMI

Body mass index (BMI) is a quick screening number made from your height and weight. Clinics and public-health bodies use it to place adults into broad groups. It is a screening number, not a medical assessment, and it cannot see muscle, bone density, waist shape or pregnancy. Think of it as one clue beside how you feel, how you move, and what your doctor sees in a fuller check.

How to calculate BMI by hand

The adult formula is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared: BMI = kg / m². Example: someone who is 170 cm tall weighs 70 kg. Height in metres is 1.70. Square that to get 2.89. Divide 70 by 2.89 and you get about 24.2, which sits in the healthy range. Prefer imperial? Convert pounds to kilograms and feet-and-inches to metres first, or use the toggle above. On this page the maths stays in your browser.

Adult BMI categories

Health Canada follows WHO adult groups. Under 18.5 is underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 is healthy weight, 25 to 29.9 is overweight, and 30 or higher is obese, with Class I (30-34.9), Class II (35-39.9) and Class III (40+). These bands describe population risk patterns. They do not name your worth or predict any single outcome.

BMI by age

For adults, the official healthy range stays 18.5 to 24.9 whether you are 25 or 75. What changes with age is how you use the number. In your late teens and twenties, building strength and walking habits pays off for decades. Through the thirties and forties, muscle and stress management often decide how steady energy feels. From fifty to sixty-four, protecting muscle and bone becomes a clear priority. After sixty-five, some research suggests a slightly higher BMI can be fine for some people, and strength plus balance usually matter more than chasing a single reading - talk with your doctor.

BMI for older adults

Later life brings shifts in muscle, appetite and medication. A lower BMI can flag unintended weight loss that needs medical attention. A higher BMI can coexist with strong legs and a healthy waist, or with little muscle and a larger waist. That is why older adults benefit from looking at strength tests, walking speed and medical history alongside BMI. If you are 50 or older and want a training plan that respects joints and balance, read Sanjeev's guidance on arthritis, osteoporosis and sarcopenia or book a consultation.

BMI and muscle

Athletes and people who lift often weigh more because muscle is dense. BMI can place them in overweight or obese categories even when body fat is modest. The reverse can also happen: someone with little muscle and more fat can look "fine" on BMI while still needing strength work. Waist measurement, progress photos, how clothes fit and performance in the gym fill in the picture. Sanjeev's article on body composition changes explains why the scale alone misleads.

South Asian cut-offs

A WHO expert consultation noted that many Asian populations can show higher metabolic risk at lower BMI values than the standard European-derived cut-offs. Action points near 23 and 27.5 are widely discussed for South Asian adults. This calculator can switch to those reference lines when you tick South Asian background. The switch does not replace blood work, family history or a clinician's judgement. It simply shows a more cautious reading of the same height and weight.

What to do with your result

Start with honesty and a small next step. If your BMI is underweight, focus on regular meals, protein and supervised strength work, and ask a doctor about unexplained loss. If you are in the healthy range, keep training and sleep consistent. If you are overweight or in an obese class, pair daily walking with two or three strength sessions and food patterns you can keep - then check medical advice before big changes. Browse fat-loss training notes, strength guidance, or book time with Sanjeev when you want a plan built around your schedule.

BMI versus waist and other checks

Waist circumference tracks abdominal fat in a way BMI cannot. Blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, sleep quality and mood all matter too. Use BMI to open a conversation, not to close one. If numbers worry you, bring them to your family doctor or pharmacist and ask what else to measure. Personal training can support movement and habits once medical questions are clear.
