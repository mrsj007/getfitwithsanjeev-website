More and more people are using medications such as Ozempic as part of managing their weight or blood sugar. Many of them ask the same thing: "How should I train now?"

This article is general education about exercise and everyday food habits. It does not give medical advice, and every decision about your medication belongs with your doctor.

## Why muscle needs attention

These medications often reduce appetite. Eating less can mean eating less protein too, and when the body loses weight without a reason to keep its muscle, a share of that weight can be muscle. Muscle matters for strength, balance, joint support and everyday energy, so it is worth protecting.

## Strength training is the signal to keep muscle

Lifting tells your body that muscle is still needed. You do not need heavy weights or long sessions to send that message.

- Aim for two to three strength sessions a week.
- Choose simple movements: sit-to-stand squats, hip hinges, rows, presses and carries.
- Start light and add a rep or a small amount of weight when it feels steady.
- Leave a day between sessions that work the same muscles.

If you are new to lifting, the first weeks should feel manageable, not exhausting.

## Make smaller meals count

When appetite is low, there is less room for food that does not pull its weight. A few habits help:

1. **Start with protein.** Eggs, Greek yogurt, fish, chicken, tofu, lentils or cottage cheese are common choices.
2. **Eat regularly even if you are not very hungry.** Small meals and snacks can add up.
3. **Add colour and fibre.** Vegetables, fruit, beans and whole grains support digestion and overall health.
4. **Sip fluids through the day.** Staying hydrated can help you feel better in training.

A registered dietitian can set targets that fit your health, your medication and your food preferences.

## Walking still counts

Daily walking supports your heart, mood and joints, and it is easy to repeat. Many people find it helps when they do not feel up to a full workout.

## Listening to your body

Some people notice tiredness, nausea or lightheadedness, especially when they eat very little. On those days:

- Lower the load or cut the session short.
- Move more slowly between exercises and stand up gradually.
- Skip the workout if you feel unwell, and rest.

If these feelings are frequent or strong, tell your doctor. They are the right person to look at what is going on.

## A simple weekly shape

- **Two or three strength days:** about 30 to 45 minutes, using full-body movements.
- **Most days:** a walk of a length that feels easy.
- **One day:** rest, stretching or gentle mobility.

Keep it simple. Consistency matters more than any single workout.

## What happens when the medication changes

Plans change. People may adjust or stop their medication under medical guidance. A steady habit of strength training and balanced eating gives you something solid to rely on whatever happens next. Keep your routine going and speak with your doctor about any change.

## When to get advice first

Talk with your doctor before starting or changing an exercise plan if you have diabetes, heart or kidney conditions, take other medications, or have had recent surgery. If eating has ever felt out of control, or you have a history of disordered eating, please check in with a health professional first.

## Where personal guidance helps

Training around a lower appetite, a changing body and a busy life is easier with a plan made for you. If you would like help building one, personalized guidance can take the guesswork out. This article is general education and does not replace advice from your doctor or a registered dietitian.
