Everyday Vitamins and Supplements: Do You Actually Need Them?

Supplementation - General Health

By Sanjeev Kumar, Level 3 Personal Trainer

Key takeaways

  • Food comes first. Supplements fill specific gaps, not general ones.
  • A blood test and a conversation with your doctor beat guessing.
  • More is not better: very high amounts of some vitamins can cause harm.
  • Tell your doctor and pharmacist about everything you take.

Walk into a pharmacy and the vitamin aisle can feel like a test you did not study for. The truth is that most people get much of what they need from food, and supplements are for specific gaps.

This article is general education. It is not medical advice, and it does not tell you what to take. Your doctor, pharmacist or a registered dietitian can do that.

Food first

A varied diet with vegetables, fruit, whole grains, protein foods and healthy fats covers most needs. Supplements cannot replace that, and they do not make up for a poor diet. Think of them as a patch for a known gap.

Who may need what

Some groups are more likely to benefit from a conversation about supplements:

  • People eating mostly plant-based foods: vitamin B12 is not reliably found in plants.
  • Older adults: absorption of some nutrients, including B12 and vitamin D, can change with age.
  • People with limited sun exposure: vitamin D is often discussed in Canadian winters.
  • People who are pregnant or planning to be: prenatal vitamins are usually advised by a doctor.
  • People with low iron: this should be confirmed by a blood test, since extra iron is not right for everyone.
  • People with digestive conditions or restricted diets: absorption or intake may be limited.

Common supplements, in plain terms

  • Vitamin D. Supports bones and muscles. Needs vary by age, skin and sun exposure.
  • Vitamin B12. Important for nerves and blood. Often recommended for vegans and some older adults.
  • Iron. Essential, but only supplement when a test shows you need it.
  • Omega-3. Fish and some plant foods provide it. Supplements are an option if you eat little fish.
  • Calcium. Food sources first. Ask before adding a supplement.
  • Multivitamin. Can cover small gaps, but not a substitute for meals.

More is not better

Some vitamins and minerals build up in the body, and very high amounts can cause harm. "Natural" does not mean risk-free. Stay within the amounts on the label and your doctor's advice, and avoid stacking several products that contain the same nutrient.

Medication and health conditions

Supplements can interact with medications, such as blood thinners or thyroid medication, and may not suit some health conditions. Tell your doctor and pharmacist about everything you take, including herbs and vitamins.

Choosing a product in Canada

  1. Look for a Natural Product Number (NPN) or a Homeopathic Medicine Number on the label. It shows Health Canada has reviewed the product.
  2. Pick simple products with clear ingredient lists.
  3. Choose independently tested products where possible.
  4. Check expiry dates and store as directed.
  5. Buy from reputable pharmacies and retailers.

A better first step

Before buying anything, ask your doctor whether a blood test makes sense, and review your meals with a registered dietitian. Fix gaps with food first, and add a supplement only for a reason you can name.

When to get advice

Speak with a professional if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, taking medication, managing a health condition, planning surgery or giving supplements to a child. Stop and seek help if you notice a reaction such as swelling, a rash or trouble breathing.

Where personal guidance helps

A plan that looks at your whole routine, from movement to meals, is easier to follow than a cabinet of pills.
